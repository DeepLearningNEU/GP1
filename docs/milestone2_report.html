1. What was built

  • Encoder: CLIP ViT-L/14 vision Transformer, frozen. Features are pre-computed once: the projected [CLS] embedding (768-d) and the last-layer patch tokens (257 × 1024).
  • Decoder: GPT-2 small (124M), fine-tuned. Captions are fed as <|endoftext|> caption <|endoftext|>; the first token acts as BOS, the last as EOS, and the loss is computed on caption tokens and EOS only.
  • Embedding injection — prefix conditioning: a 2-layer MLP maps the CLIP embedding to 10 prefix vectors (10 × 768) that are prepended to the caption token embeddings (ClipCap-style). GPT-2's architecture is unchanged; 32.5M new parameters.
  • Embedding injection — cross-attention: CLIP patch tokens are projected to 768-d (Linear + LayerNorm) and every GPT-2 block gets a new cross-attention layer over them (29.2M new parameters).
  • Data: the 5,000 COCO train2017 images from Milestone 1, now with all of their captions (25,007 image–caption pairs); 5000 COCO val2017 images (25,014 captions) for validation loss, samples and the decoding study.
  • Training: AdamW, batch 32, learning rate 0.0002 for new layers and 5e-05 for GPT-2, 300 warm-up steps then linear decay, gradient clipping 1.0. Prefix model: 5 epochs; cross-attention model: 5 epochs. Trained on the NEU Explorer HPC cluster (Tesla P100-PCIE-12GB).
  • Decoding: greedy, beam search (3 and 5 beams, length-normalized), and nucleus sampling (p = 0.5 and 0.9), implemented directly so the same code works for both injection methods.

BLEU and CIDEr are computed against the five human captions per image (pycocoevalcap, lower-cased, punctuation removed). CLIPScore = 2.5 × max(cos(image, caption), 0) with CLIP ViT-L/14, a reference-free image–text match. Distinct-2 = unique bigrams / all bigrams across the 500 captions; unique captions = share of distinct captions; repeated bigram = share of captions that repeat a bigram.

5. Summary of observations

  1. Both injection methods train quickly, then overfit. One epoch takes validation loss from 6.04 (prefix) and 4.61 (cross-attention) down to about 2.3. Validation loss is lowest at epoch 3 for prefix conditioning (2.233, perplexity 9.3) and at epoch 2 for cross-attention (2.225, perplexity 9.3), and rises after that. Cross-attention overfits much more: at epoch 5 its train loss is 1.22 but its validation loss is 2.45, while prefix conditioning ends at 1.70 / 2.27. Our explanation is that cross-attention reads all 257 patch tokens of every image, so with only 5,000 training images it can memorize them. The prefix model sees a single 768-d vector per image. All decoding results below use the epoch-5 checkpoints, so they are slightly worse than the best epoch. Keeping the best-validation checkpoint (early stopping) is the first change for the next milestone.
  2. How the captions change during training. Before training, GPT-2 ignores the image and writes generic news text, nearly the same sentence for every image (e.g. "The U.S. Department of Justice has filed a lawsuit…"). After 100 steps it has learned the short COCO caption style but collapses onto a few generic captions: with cross-attention, 5 of the 8 sample images get "A man is standing next to a woman."; with prefix conditioning, 4 of 8 get "A man is standing on a bench with a baseball bat." By step 300 the main object is usually right (bus, bed, cat, cell phone), and by the end the captions contain specific details (a tray of donuts, a bus parked next to a car).
  3. Cross-attention beats prefix conditioning on every reference-based metric. With beam-3, CIDEr is 1.085 vs 1.009 and BLEU-4 is 0.313 vs 0.292. With greedy decoding, CIDEr is 1.026 vs 0.924. Access to spatial patch features helps the decoder name objects and their relations. The cost is about 50% longer training (34 vs 23 min for 5 epochs on a P100) and about 2× slower decoding. The two methods add a similar number of new parameters (29.2M vs 32.5M), so the gain comes from the richer image representation, not from model size. CLIPScore is the same for both (about 0.61).
  4. Decoding strategy trades accuracy for diversity.
    • Beam search gives the most accurate captions. Beam-3 has the best CIDEr for both models. Beam-5 does not improve CIDEr over beam-3, makes captions more uniform (prefix: 90% unique captions vs 95% for greedy; lower distinct-2), and is about 4× slower than greedy.
    • Nucleus sampling with p = 0.9 is the most diverse: nearly every caption is unique (99.6–100%) and distinct-2 is 0.52 vs 0.28 for beam-5 (prefix model). It is also the least accurate (CIDEr 0.64 prefix / 0.75 cross), and it sometimes adds details that are not in the image, e.g. "The stop sign has been vandalized."
    • Nucleus p = 0.5 sits between the two.
    CLIPScore barely changes across strategies (0.588–0.609). Sampling mostly changes the wording and the extra details, not whether the caption matches the image.
  5. Typical errors. Similar-looking objects are confused: a person at a keyboard with a microphone is captioned as "playing a guitar" by both models. Some attributes are invented. Greedy decoding sometimes repeats a phrase ("a red headboard and a red velvet headboard"); 4–10% of captions repeat a bigram. Human references are often more abstract ("Eating a donut makes for a quick and easy breakfast."), which BLEU and CIDEr penalize even when the generated caption is correct.
